Matter

The key points about solids, liquids, gases and how we measure them. Remember: matter has mass and takes up space.

What is matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Mass

How much matter there is. Units g, kg. Measured with a balance.

Volume

How much space it takes up. Units cm³, ml. 1 cm³ = 1 ml.

Matter

  • air, water vapour
  • water, juice, milk
  • rice, sand, pebble
  • bottle, pencil

NOT matter

  • light
  • heat
  • sound, music
  • shadow
2

The 3 states of matter

Solid✓ definite shape   ✓ definite volume   ✗ cannot be compressed
pebble, pencil, plastic bottle, ice, sand
Liquid✗ no definite shape (takes the container's shape)   ✓ definite volume   ✗ cannot be compressed
water, juice, milk, oil
Gas✗ no definite shape   ✗ no definite volume (fills the whole container)   ✓ can be compressed
air, water vapour

Changing a solid's shape

Plasticine star → ball: the mass and volume stay the same. Only the shape changes.

Pouring a liquid

200 ml from a beaker into a flask: mass and volume stay the same. Only the shape changes.

Sand and sugar are solids

They can be poured, but each grain has a definite shape and volume.

A bottle is a solid

The container itself has a definite shape AND a definite volume.

3

Gases fill their container

Volume of gas = volume of its container.

  • Pumping more air into a full bottle or ball: mass increases, volume stays the same (the air is compressed).
  • Pumping air into a flat bouncy castle: the volume of air goes up until it is full, then stays the same.
  • Air has mass: a pumped-up ball is heavier than a flat one, so its side of the balance goes down.
  • A syringe of air can be pushed in. A syringe of water cannot.
4

Air takes up space

Cup pushed straight downTissue stays dry. The air in the cup occupies the space and cannot escape, so water cannot get in.
Cup tilted, or hole in the baseAir escapes, so water enters and takes its space. Tissue gets wet.
Blow air in with a strawWater level in the cup drops. The air blown in pushes the water out.
Rocks dropped into waterWater level rises. Solids occupy space.
5

Measuring mass and volume

Reading a measuring cylinder

  • Work out each small mark first: 10 ml ÷ 5 spaces = 2 ml each.
  • Read at eye level, at the bottom of the curve.
  • Always write the unit.

Volume of a stone (displacement)

final level−first level=volume
  • Example: 50 ml → 75 ml with 5 marbles: 25 ÷ 5 = 5 cm³ each.
  • The object must sink and not soak up water. A sponge does not work.

Mass of a liquid

(container + liquid) − (empty container). Example: 380 g − 200 g = 180 g.

Ordering masses on balances

The lower side is heavier. Write each fact (A > B), then chain them. Read mass from the balance and volume from the water separately.

Marbles in a box

100 marbles of 1 cm³ will not fit into a 100 cm³ box. Marbles have a definite round shape, so there are air spaces between them.

6

Watch out!

✗More air pumped in = bigger volume of air than the bottle.
✓The mass goes up. The volume = the bottle's volume.
✗A water bottle has no definite shape.
✓The bottle is a solid: definite shape and volume.
✗Sand is a liquid because it can be poured.
✓Sand is a solid. Each grain keeps its shape.
✗Light and shadows are matter.
✓They have no mass and take up no space.
✗The tissue stays dry because the cup is waterproof.
✓Air occupies the space and cannot escape.

Remember in 3 lines

Matter = has mass + occupies space
Solid: shape ✓ volume ✓ · Liquid: volume ✓ · Gas: neither, can be compressed
Gas volume = container volume. Stone volume = rise in water level.
